Why Most Sales Training Programs Fail Within Sixty Days

The sales training industry is worth billions of dollars and yet most sales managers will tell you, if you ask them honestly, that the last major training program their team completed did not stick. Reps attended, they participated, they sometimes even said they found it valuable, and then three months later the old habits were back and the numbers looked the same.

This is not a new observation. Training retention research has pointed to the same basic problem for decades. Learning that is not reinforced quickly fades. The window for reinforcement in a sales context is surprisingly short, often as little than two weeks. After that, the pressure of daily quota expectations competes directly with any new behavior the training tried to install, and quota usually wins.

What the better programs do differently is not more content, it is more structure around the time immediately after the training ends. The programs that consistently show results in our evaluations share a few specific characteristics. They require the coach or manager to participate, not just the rep. They include a reinforcement cadence that is built into the program itself rather than left to the organization to figure out. And they define success in observable behaviors, not just test scores or satisfaction surveys.

One pattern we have noticed in lower-rated programs is what we call the information dump problem. These programs try to cover everything in one intensive period, often two or three days, on the assumption that comprehensive coverage equals comprehensive learning. It rarely does. The highest-rated programs in our guide tend to be more modular, designed to be used in shorter sessions over several weeks, with coaching checkpoints built in.

If you are evaluating a training program for your team this year, we recommend asking the vendor two specific questions before you sign anything. First, what is your recommended reinforcement schedule after the initial training? Second, what role does the manager or coach play in the program design? The answers will tell you a great deal about whether the program was designed by people who understand how adults actually learn.
